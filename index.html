Iris-Lite

A surveillance prototype that records nothing until something happens. Six processes share a five-minute video buffer in RAM; when a detector fires, a C++ engine pulls that window out and encodes it to H.264, spending bitrate on the frames that earned it. Everything runs on one Raspberry Pi 4B, and nothing leaves the device.

Raspberry Pi 4B / 2 GB Python 3.12 + C++20 STEAM ICAC 2026 · Team C6 Nov 2025 – May 2026

The idea

A consumer security camera records continuously and ships the footage somewhere else. That costs storage, wears out flash, burns power around the clock, and puts your driveway on a server you don't own. Most of what it saves is an empty porch.

Iris-Lite inverts that. The camera keeps the last five minutes in shared memory and writes nothing to disk. Cheap always-on detectors watch that stream, and only when one of them is convinced something happened does the expensive work — entropy analysis, importance scoring, H.264 encoding — actually run. The output is a directory of short clips instead of a wall of tape.

Architecture

Six processes run in parallel, coordinated by two things: a POSIX shared-memory region for pixels, and a multiprocessing.connection socket on 127.0.0.1:8989 for events. Nothing passes frames over the socket — detectors send a small CaptureClass record (trigger name, start time, end time, GPIO state) and the buffer manager resolves those timestamps into frame indices.

startCamera.py
Pulls 1920×1080 MJPG from the USB camera over V4L2 and copies each frame into the iris_live_frame shared block. Also renders a live motion-diff overlay into the bottom-left 600×450 region.
main.py
The buffer manager. Re-encodes each frame as JPEG at quality 45 and writes it into a 7,200-slot ring — 24 fps × 5 minutes — with a 200 KB slot ceiling and a quality-30 retry for frames that overflow it. Listens on the event bus and maps incoming timestamps to ring indices.
detector_pipeline.py
The two-tier motion filter — a cheap always-on pass that decides whether the expensive one is worth running.
zone_monitor.py
Watches for a foreground object's feet entering a ground region it segmented at startup.
listener.py
Gates on volume, then classifies two-second windows of audio against a CNN trained for this project.
PerceptualCompressor
The C++ engine. Blocks on the iris_concern_indices block, and on a new event reads the frame window straight out of the ring and encodes it.

Each detector attaches its own read-only view of the camera block rather than receiving copies, so adding a fourth detector costs CPU but no extra memory.

Detection

Three independent detectors, all analysing at 640×360 and capped at 15 fps. Any of them can raise an event, and each one pads its window by five seconds on both ends so a clip never opens mid-incident.

Two-tier motion filter

Tier one is always on and deliberately cheap: CNT background subtraction, firing when more than 10% of the mask is non-zero, plus an exponential moving average of frame luminance (α = 0.05) that fires on a shift of more than 90 grey levels. That catches a light switch and a person equally well, which is the point — it is a filter, not a decision.

Tier two only runs on frames tier one flagged. It takes the Shannon entropy of the pixels under the motion mask and discards anything below 3.0, which rejects flat, uniform changes like a cloud passing. What survives gets centroid tracking: the largest contour's centre must move more than 20 px between frames to count, and the counter decays when it doesn't. Fifty consecutive qualifying frames raise an event. A tree in wind never gets there.

Zone monitor

On startup it segments the scene once in HSV to find ground it recognises — grass, in the tested configuration — and keeps the two largest, closest contours as the zone mask. From then on it runs GSOC background subtraction and takes only the bottom 20 pixels of the three largest foreground objects, on the theory that what matters is where something is standing, not how tall it is. Overlap between that strip and the zone mask is averaged over a 10-frame window; sustained contact for more than two seconds raises an event tagged Grass Overlap.

Audio classifier

Audio is captured at 16 kHz and gated on RMS volume (roughly 72 dB SPL) so the model isn't run against silence. Above the gate, the last two seconds are turned into a 128-band mel spectrogram — the mel filterbank and STFT are written out longhand with NumPy rather than pulled from librosa, which the Pi doesn't need to carry — and passed to a LiteRT interpreter.

The model is a CNN trained from scratch on Freesound data for this project, not a fine-tuned YAMNet. It scores seven classes; six raise events above 45% confidence, and ambience is the one that doesn't:

  • Gunshot, screaming, glass breaking
  • Aggressive knocking, car screech, dog barking
  • Ambience — ignored

GPIO

A reed switch on pin 17 and a motion sensor on pin 27 don't raise events on their own. Their state is stamped into every CaptureClass, so the compressor knows a door was open while it decides how much bitrate the clip deserves.

P.E.L.I.C.A.N.

Perceptual Edge Lightweight Intelligent Compression and Adaptive Network — the C++ half of the project, written by Shreyash Thakur. The premise is that within a ten-second clip the frames are not equally worth keeping, so quality should track content rather than being fixed at encode time.

Analysis runs on a 320×180 copy while the encoder works at 640×360. Each frame gets a score in [0, 1] from five signals:

SignalWeightHow
Motion0.30Mean absolute frame difference, EMA-smoothed 0.7/0.3, with a 0.015 noise floor
Spatial0.20Canny edge density across the frame
Region0.20Edge density in the centre half, scaled 1.35×
Temporal0.20Gaussian falloff from the event's midpoint
Face0.10Haar cascade, every 10th frame, decaying 0.85× between hits

The weighted sum is passed through log1p(x·7)/log1p(7), which lifts the low end — most frames score poorly on raw motion, and a linear map would flatten the whole clip into the worst quality tier.

Pressure

A SystemGovernor reads /proc/stat and the thermal zone and fuses them into one pressure value (0.7 CPU, 0.3 temperature). Pressure scales importance down, so the same scene encodes more cheaply on a hot, loaded Pi than on a cold idle one. Frames are only dropped outright under genuine duress — pressure above 0.96 and importance below 0.25 — because a compressor that thins footage during the busy moment defeats the purpose.

Frames go to FFmpeg over a pipe using h264_v4l2m2m, the Pi's hardware encoder, falling back to libx264 -preset veryfast where that isn't available. Because the hardware encoder takes a bitrate rather than a CRF, the tier is mapped to one — 2000 kbps at CRF 18, sliding 80 kbps per step, clamped to 500–4000.

Before any of that, EventCluster merges detections that land close together in time and carry comparable weight, so a door opening and a gunshot become one clip while a gunshot and a grass overlap stay separate. Clips are written to /mnt/clipDrive/clips/, named for their frame range and trigger.

No crashes, shared-memory faults or dropped frames over the run. RAM held between 1.5 and 1.8 GB against the 1.44 GB the ring buffer reserves outright, and the package stayed at 68 °C with passive and active cooling.

The shape of that table is the interesting part. GSOC background subtraction in the zone monitor costs more than everything else combined, and it runs constantly. The two components that only work when something happens — the listener and the compressor — idle near zero and peak at 16.1% and 10.1% respectively. The event-driven design shows up directly in the min/max spread.

Roughly 9 points of the total are cv2.imshow preview windows, which exist for demonstration and would not run on a deployed unit.

Privacy and power

No frame is uploaded, and there is no cloud account. Face detection exists only as a Haar cascade feeding one 10% term of the importance score — it answers "is there a face here," never "whose." User-defined privacy zones are excluded from processing and surface only as abstract movement.

Storage behaviour follows from the buffer: the rolling five minutes lives in RAM, which does not wear out under constant rewriting the way an SD card does, and only clips that survive detection are ever committed to flash.

Charging is handled off-board by an ESP32-C3-WROOM-02 on a custom KiCad board. It queries the Green Software Foundation's Carbon Aware SDK over Wi-Fi and gates the charge line, so the unit draws from its battery and tops up when the grid it is attached to is running clean. A status GPIO blinks on Wi-Fi or query failure so the board fails loudly rather than silently charging on dirty power.

What it doesn't do

Detection is motion- and region-based, so it can be fooled. Sudden lighting changes and fast movement across the frame both produce false positives, and a slow, careful approach that never accumulates 50 frames of centroid travel can be missed entirely.

The audio model is trained on a small dataset and its accuracy reflects that. Six classes is a narrow vocabulary for the range of sounds a porch actually produces.

The zone monitor recognises grass because grass is what it was tuned for in HSV. Pointing it at gravel or concrete means retuning the range by hand.

Compression trades quality for storage in low-activity regions by design, which means a clip's dormant background is visibly softer than its subject. That is the intended behaviour, but it is a trade, not a free win.

Adding cameras is not free either. At this CPU load a second stream needs a hardware accelerator — a Coral Edge TPU would move the neural work off the CPU — before one Pi could act as a hub.
