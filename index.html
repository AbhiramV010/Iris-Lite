Iris-Lite

A surveillance prototype that records nothing until something happens. Six processes share a five-minute video buffer in RAM; when a detector fires, a C++ engine pulls that window out and encodes it to H.264, spending bitrate on the frames that earned it. Everything runs on one Raspberry Pi 4B, and nothing leaves the device.

The idea

A consumer security camera records continuously and ships the footage somewhere else. That costs storage, wears out flash, burns power around the clock, and puts your driveway on a server you don't own. Most of what it saves is an empty porch.

Iris-Lite inverts that. The camera keeps the last five minutes in shared memory and writes nothing to disk. Cheap always-on detectors watch that stream, and only when one of them is convinced something happened does the expensive work — entropy analysis, importance scoring, H.264 encoding — actually run. The output is a directory of short clips instead of a wall of tape.

The rest of these pages document how that is put together, what the parts actually do, and where the design falls down.

How a clip gets made

The whole system is one loop repeated at different speeds. Every stage below is covered in detail on its own page.

CAPTURE
startCamera.py Pulls 1920×1080 MJPG from the USB camera and writes each frame into a single shared-memory slot that every other process reads from.
▾
BUFFER
main.py Re-encodes each frame as JPEG and drops it into a 7,200-slot ring — five minutes of history, overwritten continuously, never touching disk.
▾
DETECT
Three detectors Motion, ground-zone intrusion and audio classification each watch the live frame independently. Any one of them can raise an event.
▾
RESOLVE
Event bus A detector sends a small record over a local socket. The buffer manager turns its timestamps into ring indices and pads the window by five seconds each way.
▾
ENCODE
PerceptualCompressor The C++ engine reads that frame range straight out of the ring, scores every frame, and encodes to H.264 at a quality that tracks what the frame contains.

Three decisions that shaped everything

The buffer lives in RAM

Five minutes of JPEG frames reserve 1.44 GB of a 2 GB machine outright. That is an uncomfortable amount of memory, and it buys two things: an SD card that is never rewritten in a loop, and the ability to save the ten seconds before a detector noticed anything.

Detection is tiered, not smart

The always-on pass is deliberately dumb and cheap — background subtraction and a running luminance average. Expensive analysis only runs on frames that pass it. A detector that is right 60% of the time for almost no CPU is more useful here than one that is right 95% of the time and saturates the core.

Quality is per-frame, not per-clip

Within one ten-second clip, the frames are not equally worth keeping. The compressor scores each one on motion, edges, position in the event and whether a face is present, then picks a quality tier from that score rather than from a fixed setting.
