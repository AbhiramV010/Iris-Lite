05 · Results & limits

What it actually costs

A ten-minute continuous run on a Raspberry Pi 4B with all six processes active, sampled every 30 seconds. The interesting result is not the total — it is the shape of the distribution, which shows the event-driven design working exactly as intended and also shows where it is paying too much.

Method

Hardware
Raspberry Pi 4B, 2 GB, passive and active cooling
Duration
600 seconds, continuous
Interval
30 seconds — 20 samples per process
Instrument
log/logger.sh, summing ps -o %cpu per process name
Load
All six processes running, detectors firing naturally

Figures are per-process CPU as reported by ps, so they are shares of a single core on a four-core part. A reading of 36% means roughly a third of one core, not a third of the machine. The raw samples are in log/irisLiteLogs.csv.

Caveat

ps %cpu is a lifetime average of the process, not an instantaneous reading, so it lags real behaviour and understates short spikes. It is the right tool for comparing steady-state cost between processes and the wrong one for measuring burst latency. Nothing here should be read as a latency measurement.

The total sits at a mean of 94.8% and a median of 94.3% of one core, against four available. Individual samples ranged from 70.98 to 113.83.

Stability

Over the full run there were no crashes, no shared-memory faults and no dropped frames. Resident memory held between 1.5 and 1.8 GB against the 1.44 GB the ring reserves outright, and the package stayed at 68 °C.

Reading the numbers

The spread is the point

Look at the two event-driven components. The listener averages 4.31% and peaks at 16.10%; the compressor averages 2.11% and peaks at 10.10%. Both idle near zero and only cost anything when something happens. That ratio — roughly 4:1 between peak and mean — is the event-driven design showing up directly in the measurements, and it is the single result the project was built to produce.

A conventional continuous-recording camera would show a flat line for its encoder at something close to the compressor's peak, all day, whether or not anything happened.

Tiered detection behaves as designed

Tiered detection has the widest spread of anything measured: 0.25% to 20.00%. That is the two-tier structure visible in the data. When tier one finds nothing, the process costs almost nothing; when it promotes frames, tier two's entropy and contour work pushes it to 20%. It sits near the top most of the time because the test scene was rarely completely still.

The zone monitor is the problem

GSOC background subtraction costs 36% of a core and never stops. It is the most expensive single component in the system, more than the camera capture, and it runs at the same rate whether the lawn is empty or not.

This is a straightforward design inconsistency. The motion detector was carefully tiered so that expensive work only runs when cheap work suggests it might be worth it; the zone monitor has no equivalent, and it is the component that would benefit most. Gating GSOC behind the same cheap CNT pass that already runs in detector_pipeline.py is the obvious next change, and would likely reclaim most of that 36%.

Preview windows

Roughly nine points of the measured total are cv2.imshow preview windows, which exist for demonstration and would not run on a deployed unit. Since these measurements were taken, the preview in detector_pipeline.py has been removed; main.py and zone_monitor.py still render theirs, and both fall back to headless operation when no display is attached.

Known issues

Defects in the current build, as distinct from the design limits below. These are things that should be fixed rather than things that are inherent.

Trigger strings stop at the buffer manager

main.py writes only two indices into iris_concern_indices, but the C++ side reads a struct that also expects a 64-byte trigger string. The engine therefore always sees an empty trigger and falls back to "unknown".

Effect: every clip is named ..._unknown.mp4, and EventCluster's trigger-weight model collapses to its 0.50 default, so it can no longer tell a gunshot from a grass overlap.

Swapped GPIO fields in the zone monitor

zone_monitor.py builds its CaptureClass with isMotionSensor=check_gpio(17) and isDoorSensor=check_gpio(27) — the reverse of every other detector, where 17 is the door and 27 is motion.

Effect: sensor metadata on grass-overlap events is inverted relative to events from the other two detectors.

Per-frame CRF does not reach FFmpeg

Quality is computed per frame, but the encoder pipe is opened once per event with a fixed bitrate on the command line. Changing it would mean starting a new file.

Effect: adaptivity is fully calculated and logged, but what varies on disk is per-clip rather than per-frame.

Timestamp-to-index mapping assumes exact 24 fps

The buffer manager converts event times to ring indices by multiplying elapsed seconds by a hard-coded frame rate. If the write loop falls behind, the mapping drifts.

Effect: the extracted window sits slightly later than the real event. The ±5 s padding absorbs small drift rather than correcting it.

No process supervision

The launcher starts six terminals and exits. Nothing monitors them and nothing restarts them.

Effect: if startCamera.py dies, the other five keep running happily against a frozen frame, with no error surfaced anywhere.

Stale ring blocks survive a hard kill

startCamera.py unlinks its own block before recreating it, but the 1.44 GB ring and its metadata blocks are reattached if they already exist.

Effect: after an unclean shutdown the memory stays reserved, and the next run may read frames left over from the previous one at the edges of a window.

What it doesn't do

These are properties of the approach, not bugs. Fixing them means changing the design.

Detection can be fooled

Detection is motion- and region-based. Sudden lighting changes and fast movement across the frame both produce false positives, and a slow, careful approach that never accumulates fifty frames of centroid travel can be missed entirely. The entropy gate rejects flat global changes, but it is a heuristic and it has a threshold.

The audio vocabulary is small

The model is trained on a small Freesound dataset and its accuracy reflects that. Six concerning classes is a narrow vocabulary for the range of sounds a porch actually produces, and anything outside them lands in whichever class is nearest in spectrogram space rather than being rejected as unknown.

The zone monitor knows grass

The ground zone is found by an HSV threshold tuned for grass. Pointing the camera at gravel, decking or concrete means retuning that range by hand. The zone is also segmented exactly once at startup, so a scene that changes appearance — dusk, snow, a parked car — is not re-learned.

Compression is a trade, not a free win

Spending less bitrate on low-activity regions is the entire point, and it means a clip's dormant background is visibly softer than its subject. That is the intended behaviour. It is still a trade, and on a static wide shot with a distant subject it is a trade in the wrong direction.

One camera per Pi

At 95% of one core and 1.8 GB of 2 GB, there is no room for a second stream. A hub design needs either more memory or an accelerator — a Coral Edge TPU would move the neural work off the CPU — before one board could serve multiple cameras.

Where it would go next

  1. Gate GSOC behind a cheap first-tier pass, the way the motion detector already does. Largest single win available.
  2. Widen the event shared-memory block so trigger strings reach the compressor, which switches on semantic clustering and gives clips meaningful filenames.
  3. Derive ring indices from the timestamps already stored in iris_indices instead of assuming a constant frame rate.
  4. Re-segment the ground zone periodically, or on a large luminance shift, rather than once at startup.
  5. Add a supervisor that restarts a dead process and surfaces the failure.
